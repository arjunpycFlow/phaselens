# Margin of safety, reverse DCF and dividend safety

## Margin of safety

The gap between the price paid and the estimated value, held deliberately wide so that being wrong about the assumptions doesn't cost money. Every intrinsic-value estimate rests on assumptions about growth, margins, duration and required return; small changes move the answer a lot.

**Width scales with uncertainty, not excitement:**

- Proven 15%+ ROIC compounder with an A moat: 0–10% at a 10% hurdle, or 20–25% at a 9% hurdle. Loosen one or the other, never both.
- Stable, predictable business with a durable moat: 20–25%.
- Cyclical, competitive, levered, or turnaround: 35–40% or more.
- Value depends on a growth rate a decade out: no discount is wide enough — use a different method or a smaller position.

**Cheap is not safe.** A declining business at six times earnings is not protected: the earnings are going to fall and the multiple was pricing that in. A margin of safety is a discount to *value*, not to a ratio.

**Other margins of safety:** a strong balance sheet, a diversified customer base, a management team that has navigated a downturn — and position size, the one entirely within the investor's control.

## Why the hurdle rate matters

With a 10% required return and 3% growth after year ten, the terminal value is 1.03 / 0.07 ≈ **14.7× year-10 owner FCF**. At a 9% hurdle it is 1.03 / 0.06 ≈ 17.2×. The 10% default is deliberately conservative for wide-moat compounders that have held higher multiples for decades; a 9% hurdle is a defensible adjustment for A-moat, low-volatility businesses. State which hurdle you used, and don't also shrink the margin of safety.

## Reverse DCF

A DCF run backwards: start from today's price and solve for the growth rate the market must be expecting. It turns "what is this worth?" into "is this expectation reasonable?"

- **Inputs:** market cap, owner FCF (operating cash flow − capex − SBC), required return, terminal growth. Owner FCF is already after interest, so it belongs to shareholders and is compared with market cap, not enterprise value; subtracting debt again would count it twice.
- **Read it against:** the company's own 5–10 year FCF growth, its market's plausible growth, and how few companies have ever sustained very high growth for a decade.
- **Where it breaks:** it assumes the price means something (manias and panics make it absurd); it is sensitive to the discount rate (run 8/10/12%); it needs a stable positive FCF base (not Phases 1–2); it says nothing about quality.

## Dividend safety score (0–5)

Five inputs, each scored 0–100 on a step curve, weighted, then divided by 20.

| Input | Weight | Full marks | Scores zero |
|---|---|---|---|
| FCF payout (5-yr median of yearly ratios) | 35% | under 40% | over 100% |
| Earnings payout (5-yr median) | 15% | under 30% | over 100% |
| Interest coverage (EBIT ÷ interest) | 20% | above 15× | below 2× |
| ROIC | 10% | above 15% | below 3% |
| Dividend growth streak | 20% | 25+ years of raises | a cut in the last 5 years |

FCF payout steps: 100 under 40%, 80 at 40–60%, 60 at 60–75%, 40 at 75–90%, 20 at 90–100%, 0 above 100%. Streak points: 20 at 2 years, 33 at 5, 60 at 10, 73 at 15, 100 at 25, in between by straight line. For the other inputs, score by straight line between the zero and full-marks points in the table (PhaseLens convention). A cut in the last two fiscal years caps the whole score at 2.5.

**Labels:** ≥4 Safe · 3 to <4 Healthy · 2 to <3 Borderline · 1 to <2 At risk · <1 Unsafe.

**Sector variants:** REITs use FFO payout (full marks under 70%) and EBITDA coverage; banks lead with earnings payout (under 40%) and equity ÷ assets; utilities use a higher payout curve (under 60%) and net debt ÷ EBITDA (under 4×); MLPs lead with distribution coverage (operating cash flow ÷ distributions, above 1.5×).
