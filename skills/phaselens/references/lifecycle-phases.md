# The five lifecycle phases

Every public company sits somewhere on the same curve: revenue climbs from nothing, flattens as the market fills, and eventually rolls over. Profit starts deeply negative, bottoms early in hyper growth, crosses zero at breakeven, and peaks a phase later than revenue. The gap between those two lines explains why a company can grow fast and lose money, or barely grow and throw off more cash than it can use.

## 1. Startup
Spending more than it earns to build a product and find a market. Revenue small and lumpy; losses deepen as it hires.

- **Good looks like:** revenue growing off a small base, gross margin improving, enough cash runway to reach the next milestone without a dilutive raise.
- **Value it with:** total addressable market and price/sales. Cash-flow methods have nothing to discount.

## 2. Hyper Growth
The product has found its market; the company spends aggressively to take as much of it as possible. Losses usually peak here and start narrowing. Share count often rises because stock pay funds hiring.

- **Good looks like:** growth well above the industry, stable or expanding gross margin, customers spending more each year, losses shrinking as a share of revenue.
- **Value it with:** price/sales and price/gross profit. Earnings multiples are meaningless. A reverse DCF is only a sanity check.

## 3. Operating Leverage
Profits appear. Revenue grows faster than costs, so margins expand year after year. Often the most rewarding phase to own.

- **Good looks like:** profit growing faster than revenue, operating margin expanding, FCF turning positive and compounding, dilution slowing.
- **Value it with:** forward earnings and forward FCF multiples. Trailing multiples look expensive exactly when the business is improving fastest.

## 4. Capital Return
Mature and cash-rich. Growth slows as the market is captured; the company can't reinvest everything at high returns, so it pays dividends, buys back stock, or acquires.

- **Good looks like:** high, stable ROIC; FCF comfortably covering the dividend; a falling share count; acquisitions that earn more than they cost.
- **Value it with:** everything works — trailing and forward P/E, P/FCF, DCF and reverse DCF. Valuation is most reliable here.

## 5. Decline
Revenue and profit fade as demand shifts, technology moves on, or competitors take share. Decline can be slow and profitable for years, which is what makes it dangerous: the numbers look cheap while the business shrinks.

- **Good looks like:** nothing, on its own terms. What matters is whether the balance sheet outlasts the decline, whether assets are worth more than the market says, and whether management returns cash instead of defending a lost position.
- **Value it with:** asset value and a wide margin of safety. Every cash-flow multiple extrapolates falling earnings.

## Which method fits which phase

| Method | Startup | Hyper Growth | Op. Leverage | Capital Return | Decline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total addressable market | useful | useful | somewhat | no | no |
| Price / sales | useful | useful | useful | somewhat | no |
| Price / gross profit | somewhat | useful | useful | somewhat | no |
| Forward P/E, forward P/FCF | no | no | useful | useful | no |
| Trailing P/E, trailing P/FCF | no | no | somewhat | useful | no |
| DCF, reverse DCF | no | sanity check only | somewhat (use forward FCF) | best fit | no |
| Asset value | no | no | no | no | best fit |

## Placing a company in under a minute

- **Revenue growth over 3–5 years**, and its direction. One year tells you about the comparison, not the trend.
- **Operating margin and its direction.** Negative and deepening, negative and improving, positive and expanding, or positive and flat are four different phases.
- **What it does with cash.** Raising it, reinvesting all of it, reinvesting some, or handing it back.

A company growing 40% with negative margins and a rising share count is in hyper growth. One growing 4% with a 30% margin, a dividend and a shrinking share count is in capital return. Boundary cases deserve the most attention, because the market often still prices them for the phase they are leaving.

Companies can move backwards — a mature business that finds a genuinely new market can return to high growth. It is rare, usually doubted at the time, and a reason to reassess the phase rather than assume it is permanent.
